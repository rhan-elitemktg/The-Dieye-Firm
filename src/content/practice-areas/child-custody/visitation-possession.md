Under the Texas Family Code, what most people call “visitation” is legally referred to as “possession and access.” The non-custodial parent is typically designated as a possessory conservator, and the court determines the schedule based on one governing standard: the best interest of the child. How that standard applies to your specific circumstances is where experienced legal guidance matters most.

## Understanding Texas Visitation Laws

The **Standard Possession Order** (SPO) is the default schedule the court applies when parents haven’t reached their own agreement. Under it, the non-custodial parent typically has the child on the first, third, and fifth weekends of each month from Friday at 6:00 p.m. to Sunday at 6:00 p.m., a mid-week period on Thursdays from 6:00 to 8:00 p.m., alternating holidays, and one month during the summer. An expanded SPO allows possession from school dismissal on Friday through the start of school Monday morning. Texas law encourages parents to reach agreements that depart from the SPO when a different arrangement better serves their child, and courts can approve those agreements.

Whether the SPO fits depends on factors like each parent’s location, the child’s age, and the family’s particular dynamics. At [The Dieye Firm](/about-us/), we’re well-versed in navigating these details to help build schedules that align with legal standards and each family’s real circumstances.

## When Supervised Visitation Is Ordered

Supervised visitation is a distinct legal status that courts impose when a parent’s unsupervised contact with the child would pose a risk of harm. Courts typically reach this conclusion after findings involving neglect, abuse, domestic violence, or active substance abuse. It isn’t a permanent designation in most cases, but it carries real compliance obligations and can affect a parent’s path toward expanded access later.

The supervising arrangement can take different forms. Parents may agree on a mutually acceptable individual to monitor visits. In more serious situations, visitation occurs at a facility specifically designed for that purpose. Violations of a supervised visitation order are treated seriously by Texas courts and can result in further restrictions or loss of visitation rights entirely.

## Modifying Existing Visitation Orders

Most parents can agree to small schedule adjustments without going back to court. If one parent needs to travel on a weekend they’re entitled to possession, they can swap weekends with the other parent by mutual agreement. No court order is needed as long as both parties agree and the change is minor.

When there is a material and substantial change in circumstances, however, a court order modifying the schedule is required. A new job with unusual hours, an offshore work rotation, or the [relocation of one parent](/family-law/child-custody/relocation-case/) can all meet that threshold. Unless both parties agree to the new arrangement, the parent requesting the change carries the burden of proving it’s warranted.

## Enforcement When Visitation Is Denied

A court-ordered visitation schedule isn’t a suggestion. When the other parent consistently denies access, changes exchanges without agreement, or ignores the order, you have legal options. In Texas, you can file a motion to enforce the visitation order, which brings the violations before a judge at a formal hearing.

Documentation is critical. Keeping a detailed record of every missed exchange, denied pickup, and schedule deviation gives us the evidence base to build a strong enforcement case. One point that often surprises parents: child support and visitation are legally separate under Texas law. A parent can’t lawfully withhold court-ordered visitation because the other parent has fallen behind on [child support](/family-law/child-support/). If you’re being denied access, [contact us](/contact-us/). We represent clients in enforcement proceedings throughout Harris County and the surrounding area.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Visitation

### What Is the Standard Possession Order in Texas?

The Standard Possession Order (SPO) is the default court-ordered schedule for non-custodial parents in Texas. It isn’t rigid; courts and parents can modify it to better fit the family’s needs, including alternating holidays and extended summer periods. See the “Understanding Texas Visitation Laws” section above for a full breakdown of the SPO schedule and expanded SPO provisions.

### How Can I Modify a Visitation Order?

Modifying a visitation order requires demonstrating a material and substantial change in circumstances that warrants revising the current order. That might include a parent’s relocation, changes to the child’s school or extracurricular schedule, or significant shifts in a parent’s work hours. A child visitation lawyer in Pearland can help ensure the necessary documentation and evidence are in place to support the request. At The Dieye Firm, we assist clients with modification proceedings and tailor our approach to each family’s situation.

### What Should I Do If My Ex Violates Our Visitation Agreement?

Document every instance: dates, times, and what was denied. In Texas, you can file a motion to enforce the visitation order, which triggers a court hearing to address the violations. Keep in mind that child support and visitation are separate legal obligations; neither parent may withhold one because of the other’s non-compliance. At The Dieye Firm, we represent parents whose court-ordered access is being denied.

### How Do Holidays Affect Visitation Schedules?

Under the Texas SPO, holidays typically alternate between parents each year, so each parent has the opportunity to share significant occasions with their child. Families can agree to customize these arrangements to better reflect their traditions, helping both parents participate in important cultural or family events. At The Dieye Firm, we work with families to build holiday schedules that respect their values and make the most of their parenting time.

### What Role Does Mediation Play in Visitation Disputes?

Mediation is a private, [non-adversarial process](/family-law/mediation-vs-litigation/) in which a neutral third party helps both parents negotiate a workable agreement outside of court. It tends to be less costly and less contentious than litigation, and it gives parents more control over the outcome. For co-parenting relationships that need to stay functional over years, an agreement reached in mediation is often more durable than one imposed by a judge. At The Dieye Firm, we support mediation as a practical option when both parties are willing to engage in good faith.

## Visitation Rights for Unmarried Parents in Pearland

When parents were never married, the path to a formal possession and access order starts from a different place than in a divorce. Texas courts generally require that [legal paternity](/family-law/paternity/) be established before a father can obtain enforceable visitation rights. Without that legal recognition, a biological father has no standing to request a court-ordered schedule, regardless of how involved he has been in the child’s life.

### How Paternity Is Established

Paternity can be established two ways. If both parents agree, a voluntary acknowledgment of paternity can be signed at the hospital or filed with the state. When they don’t agree, a court order is required, typically following genetic testing. Once paternity is legally confirmed, the Texas Family Code applies to unmarried parents the same way it applies to divorcing ones. The Standard Possession Order becomes the default framework, and the same modification and enforcement rules govern the schedule from that point forward.

Unmarried fathers who have an informal relationship with their child but no legal order in place are in a vulnerable position. The other parent can relocate, restrict access, or cut off contact without violating any court order. If you’re an unmarried parent in Pearland navigating these questions, establishing your legal rights early is an important step you can take.
